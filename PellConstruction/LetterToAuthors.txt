A prime solution to Question 9 in "On odd perfect numbers with exactly one even exponent greater than 2"

Dear Professor,

I recently read your interesting paper "On odd perfect numbers with exactly one even exponent greater than 2" (arXiv:2607.19746).

Regarding Question 9 in Section 5, "Does there exist a prime x such that x^2 + x + 1 is square-full?", I found an affirmative answer using a generalized Pell equation.

Consider the prime

x = 47116128896261596331524418282573.

For this x, we have

x^2 + x + 1 = 7^3 * a^2,

where

a = 2544031832644333879196802002161.

The complete prime factorization of a is

a = 43 * 3416683 * 12353161 * 1401752363863729.

All four factors are prime. Therefore,

x^2 + x + 1
= 7^3 * 43^2 * 3416683^2 * 12353161^2 * 1401752363863729^2.

Every prime factor occurs with exponent at least 2, so x^2 + x + 1 is indeed square-full.

This example answers Question 9 affirmatively. Since x^2 + x + 1 has five distinct prime factors, it does not directly yield a two-prime-factor solution of the form sigma(x^2) = p^a * n^b considered in Table 1. For the application to restrained odd perfect numbers, it may therefore be useful to investigate the more restrictive question of whether such a prime x exists with omega(x^2 + x + 1) <= 2.

I hope you find this example useful for future revisions of your manuscript.
